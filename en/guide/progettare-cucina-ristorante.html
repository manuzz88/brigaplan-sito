How to design a restaurant kitchen: zones and routes

A restaurant kitchen works well when everything follows a route: goods come in, are stored, prepared, cooked and go out to the dining room, without going back. Here you will find how to think it through, zone by zone, before choosing the equipment. It applies to a new venue and to a refurbishment.

Where to start

Before the equipment, you need four pieces of information. They change the whole design.

  • The menu: it decides the cooking. A kitchen for pasta dishes and grilling is not the same as one that fries a lot or works mainly with the oven.
  • Covers and sittings: what counts is the dishes in the peak hour, not the seats alone.
  • The venue: dimensions, doors, windows, where the dining room is, where the flue can run.
  • Energy: gas, electric or both; the electrical power available in kW, which you find on your electricity bill or meter; whether there is already a gas connection.

The forward route

The basic rule is forward flow: goods receiving, storage and cold rooms, preparation, cooking, pass, dining room. Dirty items and waste do not cross clean ones.

  • Goods receiving and storage: near the goods entrance, so crates do not cross the kitchen.
  • Preparation: between storage and cooking.
  • Cooking: in the centre, under the hood.
  • Pass: between cooking and the dining room. It is the point the dishes leave from.
  • Wash-up area: near the way back from the dining room, in a separate room or a separate part of the kitchen.

The zones of the kitchen

  • Storage and cold: for small volumes, refrigerated cabinets and refrigerated counters are enough. A walk-in cold room is needed when volumes are high.
  • Preparation: vegetables, meat and fish in separate places, even small ones. Each has its own worktop; vegetables and fish also have their own sink.
  • Cooking: burners or induction, griddle, fryer, pasta cooker, oven. Everything that produces fumes and vapours sits under the hood.
  • Pass: a single clean point where the finished dishes arrive, with heat to keep them at temperature.
  • Wash-up area: plates on one side and pots on the other, when space allows.
  • Hand-wash basins: in the work zones, with non-manual controls.

Dirty and clean do not go through the same door

When possible, clean plates and dirty plates take two different routes.

  • Two doors: one for the dishes going out, one for the dirty plates coming back.
  • One door and a pass: usually the clean dishes go out through the pass and the dirty plates come back through the door. It is the most convenient solution, also because the dirty plates stay out of sight if the pass opens onto the dining room.
  • Pass in the wall and gas: if the kitchen runs on gas and the power is high, the wall between kitchen and dining room may have fire-resistance requirements, and the pass must meet them. Your technician checks this.

Cooking: against the wall or as an island

  • Against the wall: it takes up less space and is the normal choice in small kitchens.
  • As an island: when the venue allows it, it is the first choice. You need aisles on both sides; the cooks work on both sides and each section has its own stretch.
  • Behind the cook: a worktable and a fridge, so whoever is cooking has everything one step away.
  • These work well against the wall: pastry, starters and plating.
  • The aisles: one comfortable main aisle that reaches every zone; the others can be narrower.

The sections

A section is the workplace of one cook or of a group: first courses, main courses, starters, pastry.

  • Small kitchens: the sections are merged. First courses and main courses become one.
  • Large kitchens: each section is a self-contained block, with its own cooking, refrigeration under the worktop and a worktable behind. The cook does not go to another section to work.
  • A single pass: each section reaches it directly, without crossing the others.
  • Pastry: in busy restaurants it usually sits in the kitchen; in many others the bar handles it.

Gas or electric

There is no right choice for everyone: gas and electric should be weighed on equal terms, starting from what the venue already has.

  • Gas: you need a connection with the right capacity and ventilation of the room. Above certain power levels the safety rules change.
  • Electric and induction: you need electrical power. Look at how many kW you have available before deciding.
  • Gas and electric together: it can be done, for example gas burners and an electric oven.

Hood and flue

  • The hood sits above all the cooking appliances. Dimensions and overhangs are set by the European standard on kitchen hoods (EN 16282).
  • The flue: where it can run and where it comes out is one of the first things to check in the venue, with your technician and with the municipality.
  • A wood-fired oven and a charcoal grill need a flue of their own, separate from the hood's.

The most common mistakes

  • Choosing the equipment before the route: then there is no room for the wash-up area or the pass.
  • Wash-up area far from the way back from the dining room: the dirty plates cross the kitchen.
  • Too little refrigeration near the cooking: the cook goes back and forth to the cold room.
  • No worktop next to the oven and the fryer.
  • Burners right against an oven or a fridge, without a neutral worktop in between.
  • Corners left empty between two counters.
  • Two different heights on the same counter.
  • Deciding gas or electric without looking at the meter and the connection.
  • Forgetting the flue until the design is finished.

What the equipment design does not decide

The equipment design says where the appliances go and how the work is done. Whether the venue complies with the rules is a different matter.

  • Heights, floor areas, toilets, changing rooms and local regulations: they change from country to country and often from city to city. Your technician checks them.
  • Paperwork and permits: you usually need a licensed local technician.
