How much space does a restaurant kitchen need?

How many square metres your kitchen needs depends on what you cook, how many covers you serve and how much arrives already prepared. Here you will find the legal minimums, the rough figures from an industry manual, the standard dimensions of the equipment and a method to work it out for your venue.

First, the legal minimums

The minimums are set by the hygiene rules of the region and of the municipality. As an example, here are those of Lombardy. These are the rules of Lombardy, in Italy. In your country the rules are different: check them with a local technician and with the local authorities. The full rules are in the guide ASL and ATS rules for restaurant kitchens in Lombardy (in Italian).

  • Floor area: kitchen, storage, cold rooms and wash-up area together have at least 0.5 m² per seat, and no less than 20 m², unless the ATS (the local health authority) says otherwise (Lombardy model hygiene regulation, in Italian).
  • Height: for a restaurant, the heights in the municipal regulations usually apply: check with the municipality and with the ATS.
  • Changing room: in Lombardy 1 m² per worker present at the same time, in Milan 1.5 m² and at least 5 m²: check with the ATS.
  • It is a minimum, not a target: the legal minimum tells you when the kitchen is too small, not when it is right for your menu.

Rough figures from the manuals

An American restaurant and foodservice design manual gives these indicative values per seat (Thomas, Norman and Katsigris, *Design and Equipment for Restaurants and Foodservice*, 4th ed., Wiley, p. 96). They are US values, converted to m² and rounded.

Type of venueDining room, per seatKitchen, per seat
Fine dining1.4-1.9 m² (15-20 sq ft)0.7-0.9 m² (7-10 sq ft)
Mid-range restaurant1.1-1.7 m² (12-18 sq ft)0.5-0.8 m² (5-9 sq ft)
Banquets0.9-1.4 m² (10-15 sq ft)0.3-0.5 m² (3-5 sq ft)
  • It does not grow in proportion: the same manual warns that kitchen space does not grow in proportion to the covers. Doubling the seats does not mean doubling the kitchen (p. 94).
  • How much of the venue the kitchen takes: according to the manual, a traditional kitchen takes up about 40-50% of the venue; with many semi-finished products and frequent deliveries it can drop to 25-35% (p. 95).
  • They are orders of magnitude: they help you understand whether a venue is suitable, not to design it. In any case, the legal minimum of your country or region applies.

The dimensions of the equipment

Professional equipment comes in standard dimensions, which you find in the manufacturers' catalogues.

  • Depth of cooking lines: modular lines usually come in depths of about 60, 70 and 90 cm.
  • Widths in modules: burners, griddles, fryers and pasta cookers are sold in modular widths, for example 40, 80 or 120 cm. The line is the sum of the modules.
  • The real dimensions are those on the data sheet: before fixing the floor plan, take the dimensions from the technical data sheet of the chosen model, including the clearances the manufacturer asks for around the appliance.
  • Getting the appliances in: check doors, stairs and lifts. Ovens, blast chillers and refrigerated cabinets must get through.

The aisles

  • One comfortable main aisle that reaches every zone of the kitchen.
  • Narrower secondary aisles: where only one person passes now and then, they can be narrower.
  • Dirty and clean: the route of the dirty plates and the route of the finished dishes do not cross. Zones and routes are explained in the guide How to design a restaurant kitchen: zones and routes.

How to work it out for your venue

  1. Start from the menu and the covers: how many dishes you make in the peak hour and which cooking methods you need.
  2. Choose the equipment: cooking, refrigeration, wash-up and preparation, with the dimensions from the data sheets.
  3. Lay out the zones: goods receiving, storage, preparation, cooking, the way out for the dishes, wash-up.
  4. Add the aisles: the main one and the secondary ones.
  5. Add toilets and changing rooms: the ones for staff required by local rules.
  6. Compare with the legal minimum: if the total is below the minimum, the kitchen must be made bigger or the menu simpler.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Counting only the cooking: storage, cold rooms and the wash-up area count towards the legal minimum, and changing rooms have a minimum of their own. They all take up real space.
  • Stopping at the legal minimum without checking that the equipment for the menu fits.
  • Using dimensions from memory instead of those on the data sheets.
  • Forgetting columns, pillars, doors and windows when drawing the line.
  • Finding out too late that an appliance does not fit through the door.

How to get a free preview

Brigaplan uses AI to generate a preview of the kitchen, and the engine checks it against the rules. Have this information ready:

  • Floor plan or description: upload the floor plan of your venue, or describe it in words with its dimensions.
  • Seats and services: how many seats you have and whether you work at lunch, at dinner or both.
  • What you cook: the type of venue and the main dishes.
  • Energy and connections: gas, electric or mixed for cooking; the electrical power available in kW, which you find on your electricity bill or meter; whether there is already a gas connection. If you don't know them yet, that's fine.
  • The city: hygiene rules change from place to place.
  • What you get: a preview without dimensions, brands or codes, with the engine's warnings.

The design is done online. Local rules must be checked with a local technician.
